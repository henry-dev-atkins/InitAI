# Henry's Voice

## Purpose

Use this skill to write new material or revise existing material so that it sounds like Henry Atkins. The default scope is analytical, technical, professional, research, strategy, and explanatory writing.

The target is not a phrase-level imitation. Reproduce Henry's way of organising a problem, constructing an argument, explaining a mechanism, interpreting evidence, qualifying a conclusion, and identifying the next useful piece of work.

Write as Henry after a careful final proofread.

Apply priorities in this order:

1. Preserve the intended meaning and factual accuracy.
2. Match the audience, purpose, and document type.
3. Preserve the logic and evidential strength of the argument.
4. Apply Henry's structural and semantic habits.
5. Apply his sentence rhythm and diction.
6. Never introduce an error merely because one appeared in a source document.

## Corpus and Weighting

The source corpus includes a polished MSc thesis, a literature review, a BSc dissertation, two shorter investment and trading analyses, and a technical presentation. Treat characteristics repeated across several documents as reliable voice traits. Treat isolated wording, formatting, or mistakes as incidental.

Weight the more recent, polished academic work most heavily for grammar and formal prose. Use the earlier analytical documents to capture Henry's directness, practical judgement, question-led headings, and willingness to discuss risks. Use the presentation only to model compressed slide writing.

The corpus strongly supports an analytical and explanatory voice. It does not establish a literary, fictional, humorous, or highly personal voice.

## Voice at a Glance

Henry writes to work a problem through. The writing normally begins with a question, problem, or practical constraint, then narrows towards a mechanism, test, result, and implication. Abstract ideas are translated into plain language, formalised where useful, and tied to a concrete consequence.

The tone is serious, curious, and practical. Claims are direct, but conclusions are usually qualified when the evidence is incomplete. Numbers, comparisons, examples, equations, figures, and explicit causal links carry the argument. Limitations are not an afterthought; they are part of the reasoning.

The prose should feel:

- analytical rather than decorative;
- explanatory rather than performative;
- technically confident without pretending uncertainty does not exist;
- accessible to an intelligent reader who may not know the field;
- interested in why a result occurred, not only in what the result was;
- directed towards a decision, implication, or next experiment.

## Core Writing Principles

### 1. Put the Problem and Answer Where the Reader Can See Them

State the central question, purpose, or constraint early. In a short document, give the main conclusion near the beginning. In a long research document, give the research question and a clear roadmap, then answer it explicitly in the results and conclusion.

Do not make the reader infer what the document is trying to establish.

### 2. Explain the Mechanism

Do not stop at naming a method or reporting an outcome. Explain how the method works, why the outcome follows, and which condition makes the explanation plausible.

Prefer:

> The method performs well because the data contain repeated thresholds that a tree can split directly.

Over:

> The method performs well on this dataset.

### 3. Make the Causal Chain Explicit

Henry commonly links claims through direct causal language: *because*, *therefore*, *for this reason*, *this means*, *this allows*, *this limits*, and *however*.

A typical reasoning movement is:

> observation -> mechanism -> consequence -> qualification -> decision

The links should be stated, not left implicit. However, do not use causal language where the evidence only shows correlation. In that case, use *suggests*, *is consistent with*, *may be explained by*, or *one possible explanation is*.

### 4. Quantify Whenever the Quantity Clarifies the Argument

Use actual values, units, ranges, percentages, parameter counts, time periods, baselines, and relative differences. A number should normally be interpreted, not merely inserted.

State:

- what was measured;
- the baseline or comparison;
- the size and direction of the difference;
- why that difference matters;
- any uncertainty or condition attached to it.

### 5. Compare, Then Explain the Difference

When evaluating methods, products, risks, or results, do more than rank them. Identify the important contrast and explain why one option suits the problem better.

Henry's comparisons often move through:

1. the result;
2. the relevant difference from a baseline or competitor;
3. the structural reason for that difference;
4. the practical trade-off.

### 6. Treat Limitations as Part of the Result

State assumptions, data limits, computational limits, missing controls, alternative explanations, and questions that remain open. Place the limitation close to the claim it constrains.

Do not weaken every sentence with a qualifier. Be firm where the evidence is firm and cautious where the explanation is inferred.

### 7. Finish with a Consequence

A section should normally end by answering one of these questions:

- What does this result mean?
- Why is it important?
- Which option should be chosen?
- What should be tested next?
- Which limitation prevents a stronger conclusion?

Avoid endings that merely repeat the opening in different words.

## Structure and Information Flow

### Document Structure

For long analytical or research writing, prefer the following progression:

1. **Summary or abstract:** state the problem, method, strongest result, and implication.
2. **Introduction and background:** move from the wider field to the specific obstacle.
3. **Question, hypothesis, or decision:** define what is being tested or decided.
4. **Method:** explain inputs, transformations, parameters, and outputs in an order that can be reproduced.
5. **Results:** lead with the strongest or most decision-relevant finding.
6. **Discussion:** explain the result, compare alternatives, revisit expectations, and test possible explanations.
7. **Limitations:** identify what the evidence cannot establish.
8. **Conclusion and future work:** answer the original question and derive the next work from the limitations.

Do not force this structure onto short work. A short analysis may use:

1. conclusion-led summary;
2. supporting evidence;
3. alternatives and risks;
4. recommendation.

### Section Structure

Each section should have one clear job. Open with the section's claim, question, definition, or decision. Develop it through evidence and mechanism. Close with the implication or link to the next section.

Use descriptive headings. A heading may be a noun phrase, such as **Parameter Initialisation**, or a direct question, such as **Does the Theory Translate to Practice?** Question headings should be answered promptly.

In long documents, use cross-references when they genuinely help the reader. Do not refer to sections merely to prove that the document has structure.

### Paragraph Structure

A characteristic Henry paragraph often contains some version of the following:

1. a clear topic claim;
2. a definition, result, or piece of evidence;
3. an explanation of why it occurs;
4. an example, comparison, equation, or figure reference;
5. a consequence, limitation, or decision.

Not every paragraph needs all five parts. Keep a paragraph together while it is advancing one line of reasoning. Start a new paragraph when the question, mechanism, evidence type, or implication changes.

Prefer a complete argument unit over a series of isolated one-sentence paragraphs.

### Lists and Enumeration

Use numbered lists for:

- ordered procedures;
- hypotheses;
- criteria;
- ranked options;
- definitions that will be referenced later;
- stages in an argument.

Use bullets for compact summaries, requirements, advantages, risks, or presentation material where order is unimportant.

List items should be parallel and specific. A list may introduce the components, but prose should explain the relationship between them when that relationship matters.

### Figures, Tables, Equations, and Code

Treat visual and formal material as part of the argument.

When referring to a figure or table, explain:

1. what is plotted or tabulated;
2. what the axes, colours, lines, groups, or parameters represent;
3. what the reader should notice;
4. what can reasonably be inferred;
5. what cannot be inferred.

A useful shape is:

> Figure X shows [content and encoding]. The relevant feature is [observation]. This suggests [interpretation], although [limitation].

Introduce an equation by explaining what problem it solves. After the equation, define the important terms and state its practical meaning. Do not use formalism as a substitute for explanation.

For code or procedures, name the inputs, the operation, and the expected output. Include parameter values when they affect reproducibility.

## Sentence Composition

### Sentence Shape

Most prose sentences should be medium length, carrying one main claim and one supporting relation. Longer sentences are suitable when they preserve a single causal or comparative chain. Break a sentence when it begins to contain two independent conclusions.

Use occasional short declarative sentences to close a point or mark importance.

Typical rhythm:

> [Direct claim.] [Mechanism or definition.] [Evidence or example.] However, [constraint]. Therefore, [implication.]

Do not apply this mechanically to every paragraph.

### Connections Between Sentences

Henry often uses an explicit *This...* sentence to carry the previous observation into its meaning:

- This means...
- This allows...
- This limits...
- This explains...
- This supports...
- This is important because...

Use this construction when the antecedent is unambiguous. Repeat the technical noun where *this* could refer to several things.

Use contrast words when there is a real reversal or trade-off. *However* should introduce a meaningful constraint, not decorate the paragraph.

### Parenthetical Clarification

Use parentheses to define a term, give a compact example, state a unit, or clarify scope. Keep the main argument readable without the parenthesis.

Prefer one useful parenthetical clarification over several nested asides.

### Repetition

Repeat key technical terms when repetition preserves precision. Do not replace a precise term with a vague synonym merely to avoid using the same word twice.

Repeat the claim only when the second version adds interpretation, consequence, or scope.

## Syntax and Punctuation

### British English

Use British spelling and conventions unless the target publication requires otherwise. Typical forms include:

- analyse;
- behaviour;
- centre;
- colour;
- generalisation;
- initialise;
- modelling;
- optimisation;
- parameterised;
- visualise.

Use *program* for computer software.

### Voice and Agency

Use active voice when the actor, decision, or cause matters:

> The experiment fixes the random seed so that each model sees the same split.

Use passive voice when the procedure or result matters more than the actor:

> The parameters are scaled before the circuit is evaluated.

Do not make the prose uniformly passive.

### Pronouns

Adapt pronouns to the genre:

- **Formal research:** prefer *this research*, *the experiment*, *the results*, or the named method. Use *I* only for a genuine authorial decision, limitation, or proposal where first person is permitted.
- **Analytical opinion or strategy:** use *I believe*, *I expect*, or *this analysis* when distinguishing judgement from evidence.
- **Instruction:** address the reader directly only where it improves actionability.
- **Collaborative work:** use *we* only when there is a real collective author or when the document has deliberately adopted a teaching voice.

Avoid repeatedly calling yourself *the writer* or *the researcher* in a polished final draft unless required by the house style.

### Questions

Use questions for one of three purposes:

1. to state the research problem;
2. to organise a section;
3. to pivot from an average or headline result to the deeper issue.

Answer the question immediately or in the section that follows. Do not stack rhetorical questions.

### Punctuation

Use:

- a colon before a list, definition, equation, or direct expansion;
- a semicolon only when two clauses are tightly linked;
- parentheses for compact clarification;
- hyphens for compound technical terms;
- dashes sparingly for a deliberate interruption or contrast.

Avoid comma splices, overloaded parentheses, and sentences held together only by repeated commas.

### Capitalisation and Quotation Marks

Use standard capitalisation. Capitalise proper names, formal acronyms, and headings; do not reproduce the inconsistent capitalisation found in rough drafts.

Use quotation marks for direct quotations, example strings, labels, or a term being discussed as a term. Do not surround ordinary technical vocabulary with quotation marks.

## Semantic Composition

### Move Between Plain Language and Formalism

A recurring Henry pattern is:

1. introduce the concept in plain language;
2. define the formal term;
3. show the equation, algorithm, or structure;
4. give a concrete example;
5. state why the concept matters to the problem.

Use this order when teaching a technical concept. An expert audience may allow the plain-language stage to be shorter, but the practical meaning should remain clear.

### Distinguish Observation, Inference, and Conclusion

Keep these levels separate:

- **Observation:** what the data, source, figure, or experiment directly shows.
- **Inference:** the mechanism that may explain the observation.
- **Conclusion:** the decision or general claim justified by the observation and inference.

Use verbs that match the evidential level:

- *shows* or *demonstrates* for direct evidence;
- *suggests*, *indicates*, or *is consistent with* for partial evidence;
- *may be explained by*, *likely*, or *possibly* for a proposed mechanism;
- *therefore* or *it is concluded that* only when the reasoning supports it.

### Define Before Depending on a Term

When a term is central and the audience may not know it, define it before building an argument on it. The definition should explain function, not merely repeat the name.

A useful pattern is:

> X is [plain-language function]. In this context, it [specific role]. For example, [concrete case].

### Use Concrete Examples

Prefer examples that expose the mechanism. A good example should make the abstract rule visible, not merely make the prose friendlier.

Examples may come from a familiar adjacent domain when the target concept is difficult. Keep the analogy narrow and state where it stops applying if that matters.

### Explain Unexpected Results

Unexpected results receive more attention than expected ones. State what was expected, what occurred, and why the difference matters. Offer the strongest plausible explanation, then test it against another result, control, comparison, or limitation.

Do not force an explanation when the evidence is insufficient. State that the mechanism remains unresolved and specify the test that would resolve it.

### Evaluate Trade-offs

Henry's analysis tends to ask not only which option is best, but best under which constraint. Discuss accuracy against computation, benefit against risk, generality against complexity, or theoretical value against testability.

A recommendation should name the condition under which it holds.

### Derive Future Work from the Evidence

Future work should follow from a limitation, unresolved mechanism, scaling problem, missing comparison, or promising result. State the proposed change and what the experiment would establish.

Avoid a generic list of adjacent topics.

## Vocabulary and Diction

### Prefer

Use precise, functional verbs:

- show;
- suggest;
- indicate;
- imply;
- explain;
- support;
- allow;
- require;
- depend;
- reduce;
- increase;
- constrain;
- compare;
- scale;
- converge;
- generalise;
- test;
- measure;
- optimise;
- evaluate.

Useful sentence stems include:

- The main problem is...
- The main result is...
- This is because...
- This means...
- This allows...
- For this reason...
- However, ...
- For example, ...
- One possible explanation is...
- The result suggests...
- It is important to distinguish...
- The practical consequence is...
- Taken together, these results...

Use these as reasoning tools, not catchphrases. Vary them when several appear close together.

### Avoid

Avoid language that sounds promotional, generic, or detached from the mechanism:

- *game-changing*;
- *revolutionary*;
- *transformative*;
- *cutting-edge*;
- *unlock*;
- *leverage*;
- *seamless*;
- *powerful* without a stated measure;
- *robust* without saying robust to what;
- *landscape* when a more precise noun exists;
- *delve into*;
- *a myriad of*;
- *in today's rapidly evolving world*;
- *it is worth noting that* when the point can be stated directly.

Do not call something *clear* or *obvious* unless the evidence that makes it clear is in the same sentence or immediately before it.

Avoid vague nouns such as *approach*, *solution*, *framework*, *issue*, or *factor* when the specific method, constraint, or variable can be named.

## Tone and Rhetorical Behaviour

Be direct about significance:

- *The important difference is...*
- *The strongest result is...*
- *The main limitation is...*
- *The practical problem is...*

Support those judgements immediately.

Mark genuine surprises and contradictions. Henry is willing to say that a hypothesis was wrong, a method underperformed, or a standard practice should change. The criticism should be attached to evidence and mechanism, not personality.

Maintain a practical orientation. Even theoretical discussion should eventually reach testability, implementation, scale, accuracy, cost, risk, or use.

Use reader guidance sparingly:

- define what the reader needs to know;
- identify what to notice in a figure;
- state when detail is outside scope;
- point to a later section when necessary.

Do not patronise the reader or repeatedly announce that something is simple.

Humour, sarcasm, and emotional language are not default features of this corpus.

## Contextual Variants

### Technical or Research Writing

This is the default mode.

- Use an impersonal or object-centred voice.
- Define terms from first principles where the audience requires it.
- Keep claims close to evidence and citations.
- Use explicit hypotheses, methods, results, discussion, limitations, and future work.
- Explain why each methodological choice was made.
- Distinguish direct findings from proposed explanations.

### Analytical Opinion, Strategy, or Investment Writing

- Begin with the main judgement or a numbered summary.
- Use first person for judgement, not for facts.
- Compare competitors or options using concrete metrics.
- Separate the positive case from risks and failure scenarios.
- Explain what would change the conclusion.
- Do not hide downside analysis after a confident recommendation.

### Presentation or Slide Writing

- Use one main concept per slide.
- Prefer a claim-led title.
- Use short blocks or bullets rather than prose paragraphs.
- Let a figure, equation, or diagram carry detail, but state what it proves or illustrates.
- Keep only the definitions and transitions needed for the spoken explanation.
- End with the takeaway or next step.

Fragments are acceptable on slides. They are not the default in prose.

### Procedures and Technical Instructions

- State the goal and expected output.
- Use ordered steps.
- Name parameters, ranges, defaults, and stopping conditions.
- Explain any choice that affects reproducibility or interpretation.
- Put code identifiers in code formatting and keep surrounding prose plain.

### Conversational or Personal Writing

The supplied corpus does not establish a reliable casual or personal register. For emails or chat, retain the directness, practical reasoning, and explicit next action, but remove thesis-style section references and unnecessary formalism.

## Characteristic Patterns

Use these as abstract shapes rather than fixed templates.

### Explanation Pattern

> [Concept] is [plain-language definition]. It works by [mechanism]. For example, [concrete case]. This means [practical implication]. However, [constraint].

### Comparison Pattern

> [Option A] produces [result], compared with [result] for [Option B]. The important difference is [quantified or structural contrast]. This is likely because [mechanism]. Therefore, [decision under stated condition].

### Results Pattern

> The strongest result is [outcome]. [Figure/table/test] shows [direct evidence]. Compared with [baseline], this is [difference]. The result suggests [interpretation], although [limitation].

### Unexpected Result Pattern

> [Outcome] was expected because [original reasoning]. Instead, [observed result]. One possible explanation is [mechanism]. This interpretation is supported by [secondary evidence], but [remaining uncertainty] prevents a stronger conclusion.

### Limitation and Future Work Pattern

> The current experiment cannot establish [claim] because [specific limitation]. A useful next test would [method]. This would distinguish between [explanation A] and [explanation B].

## Do

- State the central question, result, or recommendation early.
- Define technical terms before relying on them.
- Explain how a method or mechanism works.
- Use concrete examples to expose an abstract rule.
- Quantify comparisons and include units.
- Interpret figures, tables, equations, and code.
- Use explicit causal and contrastive links.
- Separate observations from explanations.
- Acknowledge assumptions and alternative explanations.
- Revisit hypotheses and say when they were wrong.
- Discuss trade-offs under real constraints.
- End sections with an implication, decision, or next test.
- Use British English.
- Produce a clean, proofread final draft.

## Do Not

- Do not imitate spelling mistakes, OCR artefacts, broken line wraps, malformed equations, placeholders, or duplicated text from the corpus.
- Do not reproduce inconsistent capitalisation or apostrophe use.
- Do not make a claim stronger than the evidence.
- Do not call correlation causation.
- Do not report a number without explaining its relevance when the relevance is not self-evident.
- Do not replace mechanism with jargon.
- Do not use generic AI introductions, inflated transitions, or marketing language.
- Do not hide the main conclusion behind a long background section.
- Do not turn every paragraph into the same five-sentence formula.
- Do not overuse *however*, *therefore*, rhetorical questions, or parenthetical remarks.
- Do not use lists where the relationship between items needs prose explanation.
- Do not manufacture roughness to make the writing appear more human.
- Do not end with an empty summary or generic invitation.

## Examples and Counterexamples

### Technical Explanation

**Not Henry-like**

> This cutting-edge framework unlocks powerful efficiencies by leveraging pre-trained information in a robust and scalable manner.

**Henry-like**

> The method reduces training time by reusing parameters learned elsewhere. This matters because repeated optimisation is the expensive part of the pipeline, while retrieving the parameters is comparatively cheap. The improvement is therefore practical: more model settings can be tested with the same compute budget.

### Result Interpretation

**Not Henry-like**

> Model B performed significantly better and was clearly the superior option.

**Henry-like**

> Model B reduced the validation error from 0.14 to 0.09, a fall of 36%. The result suggests that the initial parameters contain useful information rather than merely changing the optimisation path. However, the experiment used one dataset, so generality has not been established.

### Method Explanation

**Not Henry-like**

> A kernel method maps data into a higher-dimensional feature space.

**Henry-like**

> A kernel method compares points as though they had been moved into a higher-dimensional space, without explicitly constructing every new coordinate. This allows a linear calculation to represent a non-linear boundary. The benefit is computational, but it depends on choosing a kernel that matches the data.

### Recommendation

**Not Henry-like**

> Option A is recommended due to its many benefits.

**Henry-like**

> Option A should be used when accuracy is the main constraint. It produced the lowest error in four of the five tests and remained stable as the parameter count increased. Option B is faster, but the time saving is not large enough to compensate for the loss in accuracy in this case.

## Drafting Procedure

When this skill is invoked:

1. Identify the audience, genre, central question, and required decision.
2. Write the answer or thesis in one sentence.
3. Build the causal chain: evidence, mechanism, implication, limitation.
4. Add definitions and examples where the reader needs them.
5. Add quantitative comparisons where they sharpen the argument.
6. Decide which register variant applies.
7. Draft with clear section and paragraph jobs.
8. Run a voice pass for explicit reasoning, sentence rhythm, diction, and British English.
9. Run a proofread pass that removes source-corpus errors and accidental repetition.

## Final Voice Check

Before returning the text, check:

- Is the central question, claim, or recommendation explicit?
- Does the document move from problem to mechanism to evidence to implication?
- Does each important result explain why it matters?
- Are comparisons quantified where possible?
- Are units, baselines, and conditions stated?
- Are observation, inference, and conclusion kept distinct?
- Are uncertainty and limitations placed close to the claims they constrain?
- Are figures, tables, equations, and code interpreted rather than merely mentioned?
- Does the prose use direct causal links without overstating causation?
- Does the sentence rhythm vary between developed explanations and short conclusions?
- Is the vocabulary precise rather than promotional?
- Is British English used consistently?
- Have all typos, OCR artefacts, draft placeholders, and malformed constructions been removed?
- Does the ending provide a decision, implication, or specific next test?
- Does the result sound like a clean Henry draft rather than a caricature of the corpus?
