# Scientific Literature Review

Produce an evidence-based answer that a reader can trace to the underlying studies. Match the review method to the question and the work actually performed.

## Define the review

Frame the question, choose narrative, scoping, or systematic review methods, and set eligibility criteria. Use PICO (population, intervention, comparator, outcome) when appropriate; for technical work, specify the task, method, baseline, data, and evaluation measure.

Plan searches in relevant sources such as PubMed, arXiv, and Semantic Scholar. Record queries, dates, filters, and results. Deduplicate records, screen titles and abstracts, then assess eligible full texts. Extract study details into a consistent evidence table. Synthesize by theme, explain conflicting findings, identify gaps, and assess confidence. Verify citation metadata and DOIs before reporting.


## Make the scope explicit

Use the user's question and constraints to establish the field, date range, population or system, relevant study designs, and required output. State reasonable assumptions; ask only when a missing choice would materially change the review.

- Use a narrative review for a focused explanation or background section.
- Use a scoping review to map concepts, methods, and the distribution of available work.
- Use a systematic review only when the search, selection, extraction, and appraisal process supports that description.

Do not label a limited web search as comprehensive. If access, time, or available databases constrain the work, state the actual coverage.

## Keep the search reproducible

Build queries from the question's main concepts, synonyms, and field-specific vocabulary. Adapt syntax to each database. Search more than one relevant source when broader coverage is required, and follow references or citing papers where useful.

Maintain a search log:

| Source | Search date | Exact query | Filters | Records retrieved | Records assessed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

Record actual counts. Distinguish a database's estimated result count from the records exported or screened. Note any query changes and access failures. Never claim to have searched a source that was unavailable.

## Screen and extract

Give each record a stable identifier. Merge duplicate records while retaining their source history. Link preprints, final publications, and multiple reports of the same study so they do not become independent evidence by accident.

Apply the stated eligibility criteria consistently. Record a reason for each full-text exclusion. Mark unavailable papers separately from studies excluded for substantive reasons.

For included work, capture the details needed to interpret the result:

| Field | Record |
| --- | --- |
| Identity | Title, authors, year, venue, DOI or stable URL |
| Publication status | Peer-reviewed paper, preprint, correction, or retraction notice |
| Research setting | Population, sample size, dataset, task, and relevant context |
| Method | Design, intervention or algorithm, comparator, and evaluation protocol |
| Findings | Relevant outcomes, units, effect sizes or scores, and reported uncertainty |
| Limitations | Bias risks, missing information, and limits on applicability |
| Evidence location | Page, section, table, or figure supporting the extracted claim |

Read the relevant full text before drawing conclusions about detailed methods or results. Clearly label abstract-only evidence. Write “not reported” for missing information instead of inferring a value.

## Appraise and synthesize

Compare the strength of the evidence, not just the number of papers supporting a claim. Consider whether designs, samples, measures, and comparison conditions make results comparable. In technical reviews, inspect dataset overlap, leakage, evaluation splits, baseline fairness, and resource differences before ranking methods.

Group findings around the research question. Explain agreements and disagreements using observable differences in study conditions. Separate the authors' claims, reported observations, and your interpretation.

Assign confidence to conclusions with a stated rationale. If using informal labels such as high, moderate, or low, describe them as qualitative judgments rather than scores from a formal assessment tool. Do not equate an absence of located evidence with evidence that an effect is absent.

Perform a meta-analysis only when requested or justified by compatible data and an explicit analysis plan. Do not average incomparable metrics or treat related reports as independent samples.

## Verify and report

Verify bibliographic details against publisher pages, DOI records, or authoritative repositories. A resolving DOI alone does not establish that the linked paper supports a claim. Ensure every citation supports its surrounding statement, and check for corrections or retractions where relevant.

Structure the deliverable around:

1. Research question, review type, scope, and search cutoff.
2. Search, selection, extraction, and appraisal methods actually used.
3. Study characteristics and a comparison of the evidence.
4. Findings organized by theme, including conflicting results and confidence.
5. Evidence limitations, review limitations, and specific remaining questions.
6. References and the search or screening records needed to audit the work.

For a systematic review, use the PRISMA 2020 checklist to check reporting completeness. Report real selection counts, reviewer roles, automation, protocol amendments, and availability of materials. Never imply independent screening, registration, or formal appraisal that did not occur. Treat PRISMA as reporting guidance, not proof that the review was conducted correctly.

Finish by checking that the conclusion answers the question, acknowledges uncertainty, and makes no stronger claim than the evidence permits.
